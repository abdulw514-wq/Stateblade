How to Research YouTube Niches for Free (2026 Guide)

A step-by-step method for finding low-competition YouTube niches, validating them with real data, and understanding who's winning before you record a single video.

By StateBlade Updated August 2026 10 min read

Before you spend months creating content for a niche, you need to know two things: Is there an audience? And can you actually compete? YouTube niche research answers both — and you don't need a paid tool to do it.

This guide walks through a free, repeatable process for evaluating any YouTube niche using live channel data. By the end, you'll know exactly who's winning the niche you're considering, what keywords they rank for, and whether there's a gap worth targeting.

What is YouTube niche research?

YouTube niche research is the process of evaluating a topic area to understand its competitive landscape before you commit to creating content. Good niche research tells you:

  • How many channels are already competing in the space
  • How large and engaged those channels are (subscriber count, view totals)
  • How frequently top channels publish
  • What keywords and tags the leading channels use to rank their videos
  • Whether there are underserved sub-niches with lower competition

The goal isn't to avoid competition entirely — some competition signals a real audience exists. The goal is to understand the competitive landscape well enough to position your channel strategically.

Step 1: Start broad, then narrow

New creators often make the mistake of starting too narrow ("keto recipes for busy moms over 40 in the Pacific Northwest"). The problem is you need data to know where the gaps actually are — you can't just guess.

Start with your broad topic. If you want to make cooking content, begin there. Use a tool like StateBlade to search "cooking" and see who's ranking. Look at the subscriber counts and upload frequency of the top 10 channels. That's your competition ceiling — the most successful channels in the space.

Then run searches on sub-niches: "budget cooking", "one pan meals", "college cooking", "meal prep". Compare the channel rankings in each sub-niche. You're looking for niches where:

  • The top channels have fewer than 500K subscribers (more achievable to compete)
  • Upload pace is moderate (not daily — that's hard to match)
  • Total view counts are strong relative to subscriber counts (signals engaged audience)

Pro tip: A niche where the top channel has 200K subscribers and uploads twice a week is far more attractive than one where the leader has 3M subscribers and publishes daily. Look for the gap between audience size and creator output.

Step 2: Check the channel stats that actually matter

Subscriber count is the most visible metric, but it's not the most useful. When you're doing YouTube niche research, focus on these channel stats instead:

Views-to-subscriber ratio

A channel with 100K subscribers getting 50K views per video has a healthy 50% ratio. A channel with 500K subscribers getting 20K views per video is in decline. High ratios mean an engaged, active audience — the kind you want in your niche.

Upload frequency

StateBlade shows upload pace for every channel. A niche where top creators publish 3–4 times per week is harder to break into than one where they publish weekly. This directly affects how much content you need to produce to compete.

Channel age vs. growth

A channel that built 200K subscribers in 18 months is growing fast. A channel that built 200K subscribers over 8 years is coasting. Fast-growing channels signal a niche that YouTube's algorithm is actively promoting — good news for newcomers.

Step 3: Steal the keywords your competitors rank for

This is the most underused step in free YouTube niche research. Every channel's top videos use specific tags that tell YouTube (and you) what the video is about. These tags are effectively a keyword map of what works in that niche.

In StateBlade, click any channel result to open the channel detail view. You'll see the tags used across that channel's top-performing videos. These are your starting keywords — terms that YouTube has already confirmed are relevant to that niche and driving views to those videos.

Look across 3–5 top channels in your niche and collect their tags. Group similar ones together. The tags that appear across multiple top channels are the core keywords of the niche. The tags that only one channel uses might represent an opportunity — a sub-topic with less competition.

Step 4: Evaluate sub-niches for entry points

Once you understand the main niche landscape, use your keyword list to search for sub-niches. Take the tags you collected and run each one as its own StateBlade search. You're looking for terms where the top channels are smaller — under 100K subscribers — but videos are still getting strong view counts.

This is how you find your entry point: a sub-niche with real demand but lower competition, where you can establish authority before expanding into the broader space.

Step 5: Validate with search intent

Channel stats tell you about the supply side. To understand demand, think about search intent. People searching "how to meal prep for a week" have a specific, actionable problem. People searching "cooking" have vague interest. The more specific and actionable the search term, the higher the intent — and the easier it is to rank for.

Look at the titles of top-performing videos in your niche (not just channel stats). Patterns in video titles reveal what questions people are actually searching for. If you see the same question framed slightly differently across multiple top channels, that question is worth answering.

Common mistakes in YouTube niche research

  • Choosing based on passion alone. Passion matters, but a niche with no audience has no future. Validate demand before you commit.
  • Avoiding all competition. Zero competition often means zero audience. Some competition is healthy — it proves the market exists.
  • Researching once and never revisiting. Niches evolve. Run your niche research again every few months to spot new sub-niches emerging.
  • Ignoring upload frequency. You can't outcompete a channel publishing daily if you can only manage weekly. Choose a niche where your output pace is competitive.
  • Looking at subscriber count only. A declining channel with 1M subscribers is weaker competition than a rising channel with 100K. Views, engagement, and growth trajectory matter more.

How to research YouTube niches for free — the full checklist

  1. Search your broad topic in StateBlade and note the top 10 channels
  2. Record subscriber counts, view-to-sub ratios, and upload frequency for each
  3. Click into each channel to collect their top video tags
  4. Run searches on sub-niches using the tags you collected
  5. Identify sub-niches where top channels have under 100K subscribers but strong views
  6. Look at video title patterns to understand search intent
  7. Choose your entry point: the sub-niche with the best ratio of demand to competition
